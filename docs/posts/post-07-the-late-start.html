The Late Start

Sep 2026 · 5 min read

In the 2010–11 kindergarten cohort, boys got redshirted — held back a year before school even started, not after failing anything — at 7.3%, against 5.2% for girls (Reeves, 2022). It wasn't spread evenly, either. Private-school families did it at 8.4%. Parents with a bachelor's degree did it at 8.2%. Both comfortably above the 6% national average. Nobody in either group was panicking. They were reading the room.

What room, exactly, is the more interesting question — and the answer doesn't get simpler as the story moves from kindergarten to a college diploma. Two entirely different people can look at the same set of numbers, running from age five to age twenty-two, and walk away with opposite explanations: one says the pipeline was never built for how boys actually develop, the other says this is what happens when nobody expects much of boys anymore. Both have a case. Neither one, on its own, explains all of it.

Start with the part that isn't really in dispute. On average, girls begin puberty about a year to two years before boys do — a gap consistently documented across national health data, not a single contested study (NICHD, 2021). That's not a claim about brains being "better" or "worse." It's a claim about timing, and timing turns out to matter enormously in an institution that sorts children by birth year and expects a fixed level of self-control, sitting-still, and homework compliance from all of them at once. Richard Reeves has built a policy argument out of exactly this: default every boy into starting kindergarten a year later than every girl, not as a remedial measure for kids who are struggling, but as the standard (Reeves, 2022). Redshirting already happens informally, and unevenly — mostly among families with the money and information to notice the gap and route around it. Reeves is arguing to make the workaround the rule.

The same asymmetry shows up again once boys are old enough to be teachers instead of students. The share of male teachers has fallen from a third of the profession in 1980 to under a quarter today, and Reeves estimates that if half of middle-school English teachers were men, the reading gap between boys and girls would close by roughly a third (Reeves, 2023) — his own projection, not a separately confirmed figure.

The gap doesn't close by the time anyone's picking a college. It widens. In 2022, 66% of female high school graduates enrolled in college that fall, against 57% of male graduates, and by 2021 there were 2.4 million more women than men sitting in undergraduate classrooms nationally (Reeves & Secker, 2024). The ones who do enroll don't finish at the same rate, either — 54% of women complete a four-year degree in four years, against 43% of men, a gap that narrows only slightly by year six (68% vs. 61%) (Reeves & Secker, 2024). None of this is a recent flip, and it isn't a smooth one either. Men earned 57% of bachelor's degrees in 1970. Women reached near-parity by 1980, kept climbing through the 1990s, then plateaued for most of the 2000s before climbing again — landing at 59% of all bachelor's degrees by 2021–22, against 41% for men (NCES, 2022).

Ask people why they left, or never started, and the numbers are more interesting than a clean split. Affordability is the single most commonly cited reason for either sex — 44% of women and 39% of men name it as a major factor, close enough that it isn't really the dividing line people assume. The real divide shows up in the more personal reasons: men are more likely to say they simply didn't want a degree (34% vs. 25% of women) or didn't need one for the career they wanted (26% vs. 20%) (Parker, 2021). That's a real gap, not rounding error. What it doesn't settle is what "didn't want one" is actually made of — a calculated, confident choice, or the exit-survey version of having already checked out for reasons the questionnaire never asked about. Self-report can't tell those two apart. Neither can we, from here.

Garrett Hardin made a version of this argument in 1968, about a completely different shared-resource problem: appealing to individual conscience doesn't scale, because waiting for enough people to simply want something more never moves a population-level number (Hardin, 1968). Applied here, that's the case for redshirting boys as a default policy, hiring more male teachers, rebuilding aid and advising around the students already walking away — fix the pipeline, not the boy.

The counter-argument doesn't require throwing out any of that data. Jordan Peterson would call it a matter of voluntary confrontation — choosing a hard, multi-year commitment for a future payoff is work no institutional fix can do on a boy's behalf, no matter how badly the system is stacked against him. Agency still counts, even inside a broken pipeline. It's also true that the brain doing that choosing is still under construction — total brain volume peaks around age 10.5 in girls and 14.5 in boys (Lenroot et al., 2007), and the prefrontal cortex, the part handling planning and impulse control, isn't fully online for anyone until the mid-twenties (Sapolsky, 2017) — plateauing somewhat later in males than in females, on top of that (Heitzeg, Hardee, & Beltz, 2018). An unfinished brain isn't an excuse. It's also not nothing.

Neither argument, alone, explains the whole gap. The structural case is right that individual effort was never going to move a number this size by itself. The agency case is right that no institutional fix erases what an individual boy still has to choose, one decision at a time. What's missing isn't a winner between the two. It's a way to work the problem from both ends at once — fix what's fixable in the pipeline, and help boys build the tools and confidence to meet it anyway. Where that actually lands is genuinely still an open question — one worth sitting with, and working on, from both sides.
