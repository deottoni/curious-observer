Missing From Work

Sep 2026 · 5 min read

Male labor force participation has fallen from 87% in 1948 to 68% today (Smith & Hawrami, 2026). Some of that is just an older population — more men are retired now than in 1948. Most of it isn't. Not unemployed — out. Not counted as looking, not counted as trying, not counted at all. Ask why, and the easy answer is that these men just don't want to work anymore. The data doesn't really back that up.

Two different stories compete for what's actually happening underneath that number. One says the work itself changed faster than men could adapt to it. The other says something broke first — health, structure, the will to keep looking — and the missing job is a symptom, not the cause. Both keep turning up in the same data, sometimes in the same sentence.

Start with the jobs. Between 1990 and 2014, U.S. regions hit hardest by the collapse of manufacturing jobs to import competition saw young men's employment and earnings fall, idleness and premature mortality rise, and marriage and fertility drop alongside them — tracked with a natural-experiment design built specifically to separate cause from correlation, not just note that two trends moved together (Autor, Dorn, & Hanson, 2019). Automation tells a similar, separate story. Every additional robot per thousand workers cuts the employment-to-population ratio by roughly 0.2 percentage points and wages by 0.42% — and the effect lands harder on men, concentrated in manufacturing and other routine, blue-collar work (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2020). Two different mechanisms, same direction, hitting the same kind of job.

Where new jobs are actually showing up tells the other half of it. Richard Reeves estimates that HEAL jobs — health, education, administration, literacy — will add three new jobs for every one new STEM job by 2030. That's his own projection, not an independently confirmed number. What is confirmed: men's share of HEAL jobs has fallen from 35% in 1980 to 26% today (Reeves, 2022).

Ask the men who left why, and "don't want to" barely shows up. As of 2022, 39.5% of prime-age men outside the labor force cite disability or illness as the reason (Bengali, Duzhak, & Zhao, 2023). Alan Krueger, tracing opioid prescriptions county by county, estimated that rising prescribing between 1999 and 2015 could account for as much as 43% of the decline in men's labor force participation over that same period (Krueger, 2017) — correlational, he was careful to say, not proof of a single cause. Whatever "chose not to work" is supposed to mean here, it isn't obviously a clean preference sitting next to a pile of open jobs.

What follows the job, for the men who stay out, isn't just less income. Long-term unemployment raises the risk of a man losing contact with friends and family specifically — a pattern that shows up for men, not women, and isn't mainly about money (Eckhard, 2022). That's a narrower thing than loneliness in general, though: a separate review of 37 studies found unemployment raises loneliness broadly, in men and women about equally (Morrish & Medina-Lara, 2021). Different question, different answer — losing touch with people looks male-coded here; feeling lonely overall doesn't, at least not yet.

The structural case doesn't need much dressing up at this point. Two independent mechanisms, two different research designs, land on the same population moving in the same direction. Whatever else is true, a large share of this was never a matter of individual character.

Some men really don't want to work. That's true, and worth saying plainly. But at the scale we're talking about here — millions of men, not a handful of cases — that's not the bigger story. The bigger story is addiction, chronic pain, mental health, and loneliness showing up first, and looking like "he doesn't want to work" from the outside, when a closer look says otherwise. There's a smaller, quieter piece that might be closer to real preference: Jordan Peterson would frame it as a status problem before an employment one — a man protecting his place in a hierarchy by staying out rather than taking a visible downgrade into HEAL-type work. That's plausible.

Neither side fully explains it, but they don't carry equal weight going in. The structural case — trade, automation, a labor market reshaping itself faster than the men in it could — has real, independently designed research behind it. The agency case is real too, just smaller and harder to pin down than the popular version of it. What isn't in dispute is the cost of getting the mix wrong: men losing contact with the people around them, not just the paycheck. That's worth taking seriously either way — fixing what's fixable in the market, and rebuilding the parts of a man's life a job used to hold together almost by accident.
